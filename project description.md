2. Nigeria-first Last-mile Logistics and Dispatch Operating System
Region: Nigeria first (initially Lagos, Abuja, Kano, Ibadan; designed for African expansion)
Product thesis
Nigerian merchants, distributors, pharmacies, restaurants, field-service teams, and courier operators lose margin because orders arrive through fragmented channels, addresses are ambiguous, dispatch is coordinated by calls and chat, informal fleets are hard to control, and proof of delivery, cash reconciliation, and exception handling are weak. Build a multi-tenant operating system—not another consumer delivery marketplace—that turns an order into a dispatchable job, uses landmark-plus-map location capture and low-bandwidth driver workflows, assigns work across owned, contracted, and third-party fleets, and closes the loop with ETA, evidence, COD settlement, and SLA analytics. Differentiated wedge (design judgment): Nigeria-specific address/landmark handling, WhatsApp/SMS-assisted customer contact, offline-first driver app, configurable cash and transfer reconciliation, and an integration/API layer that lets existing businesses keep their storefront and payment provider.
Target users
•SME and mid-market retailers, social-commerce sellers, and distributors needing reliable same-city delivery without building fleet software
•Pharmacies, clinics, restaurants, and other time-sensitive operators needing dispatch visibility, customer communication, and delivery evidence
•Courier, 3PL, and fleet companies needing multi-client order intake, driver allocation, pricing, settlements, and SLA reporting
•Enterprise field-service and FMCG teams managing recurring routes, proof of service, and contractor performance
Core workflows
•Merchant onboarding: configure service zones, delivery windows, rate cards, COD/transfer rules, users, vehicles, and escalation policies.
•Order capture and normalization: dashboard, CSV, REST/webhooks, Shopify/WooCommerce-style connectors, and WhatsApp-assisted intake map each order to a canonical delivery job.
•Address and location resolution: capture phone, landmark, plus code/geolocation, delivery notes, recipient availability, and a confidence score; allow driver/customer correction without silently changing the original order.
•Dispatch planning: batch jobs by zone/time window, assign owned or contracted drivers, sequence stops with constraints, and support manual override plus re-dispatch after failed attempts.
•Driver execution: Android-first offline queue with pickup/arrive/attempt/delivered states, navigation handoff, masked calling/chat, OTP or signature/photo proof, COD amount confirmation, and incident codes.
•Customer and merchant visibility: status page and SMS/WhatsApp notifications for confirmation, ETA, delay, reschedule, failed attempt, and delivered proof; merchant dashboard exposes exceptions rather than only a map.
•Closeout and learning: reconcile COD and digital payments, calculate driver/partner payouts, manage returns, disputes, refunds, and damage incidents, then report on on-time rate, first-attempt success, cost per stop, route utilization, and zone-level failure reasons.
Production architecture
•Frontend: responsive React/Next.js operations console with role-based views; Android Kotlin/React Native driver app using encrypted local SQLite/Room queue, background sync, retry-safe state transitions, and low-data UX; lightweight customer tracking page.
•Backend: versioned TypeScript/Go service layer with tenant, order, dispatch, routing, notification, settlement, and reporting domains; REST plus webhooks, idempotency keys, outbox pattern, and explicit order/stop state machines.
•Data: PostgreSQL with PostGIS for tenants, jobs, zones, geospatial search, and audit records; Redis for short-lived locks, rate limits, and dispatch caches; object storage for delivery photos/signatures; append-only event stream for operational history; warehouse/read replica for analytics.
•Routing and integrations: pluggable map/geocoding/routing adapter with fallback providers; SMS/WhatsApp provider; payment gateway/bank-transfer reconciliation adapter; commerce/accounting/ERP connectors; partner-courier API; webhook signing and replay controls.
•Security and privacy: OIDC/JWT with MFA for operators, short-lived driver tokens, tenant-scoped authorization, least-privilege service accounts, encrypted secrets, TLS and encryption at rest, PII minimization/retention, consent and data-subject workflows, immutable audit logs, and Nigeria Data Protection Act/NDPC compliance review.
•Reliability: queue-based asynchronous dispatch/notifications, transactional outbox, idempotent consumers, dead-letter queues, exponential backoff, circuit breakers around maps/payments/messaging, bulkheads by tenant, backups with tested restore, and documented RPO/RTO.
•Observability: structured correlated logs, metrics for sync lag, assignment latency, notification delivery, API errors, route cost, and reconciliation mismatch; distributed traces; SLOs and alerting; redacted replayable event timeline for support.
•Deployment: containerized services on managed Kubernetes or a managed container platform in a Nigeria/Africa-capable region, managed PostgreSQL/Redis/object storage, infrastructure as code, CI/CD with migrations, feature flags, canary/rollback, separate staging, synthetic checks, dependency health checks, and disaster-recovery runbooks.
Monetization
•Tiered SaaS subscription by active operating users, locations, and included monthly jobs; charge extra for analytics, audit retention, and advanced controls.
•Usage fee per completed delivery or per dispatch job, with transparent pass-through for SMS/WhatsApp, maps, and payment-provider costs.
•Enterprise/API plan with onboarding, SSO, data-retention controls, SLA, dedicated support, and custom ERP/partner integrations.
•Optional payments and settlement add-ons (COD reconciliation, partner payout automation, invoice collection) priced as a small fixed fee or percentage only where legally and contractually appropriate; do not hold customer funds without the required license/regulated partner.
Why now
Sourced facts: the U.S. Commercial Service reports that Nigeria’s logistics sector is growing with e-commerce and digital logistics applications, but still faces poor roads, unreliable electricity, outdated port/customs systems, and high operating costs; it specifically identifies demand for GPS tracking, fuel management, cargo-management systems, last-mile visibility, and integrated mobile payments. The same guide projects Nigerian e-commerce revenue of $8.53B in 2024 to $14.92B in 2029 (11.82% CAGR). Nigeria’s digital base is large: the U.S. Commercial Service, citing NCC/NBS, reports about 157M mobile subscribers, more than 163M internet users as of March 2024, and 43.5% broadband penetration. CBN Payments System Vision 2025 explicitly prioritizes electronic-payment adoption, safety/resilience, an open-banking roadmap with local APIs, interoperability, stronger authentication, and dispute resolution. NDPC publishes the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 and compliance resources. The World Bank’s Nigeria diagnostic describes heavy freight traffic worsening congestion, poorly organized logistics, infrastructure gaps, flooding/climate disruption, and informal/paratransit fragmentation in Lagos, Abuja, and Kano. Design judgment: these conditions favor an asset-light control plane that improves utilization and evidence before attempting to own vehicles; low-bandwidth/offline execution and privacy/security should be first-class rather than later hardening.
Risks and controls
•Address ambiguity, unreachable recipients, and failed attempts: require landmark/phone/geo capture, confidence scoring, customer confirmation, delivery windows, driver correction with audit trail, attempt reason codes, and zone-specific playbooks.
•Poor connectivity, device battery, and GPS quality: offline-first state machine, local encrypted queue, compressed payloads, SMS fallback for critical events, sync-lag monitoring, and manual dispatch mode.
•Cash-on-delivery fraud, leakage, and payment disputes: OTP/evidence, amount lock, dual reconciliation against gateway/bank statements, driver cash limits, exception queues, segregation of duties, and regulated payment partners.
•Driver safety, theft, harassment, and liability: vetted partner onboarding, masked communications, panic/escalation procedure, time/location audit, restricted high-risk zones/time windows, incident reporting, and appropriate insurance/contract terms.
•Privacy, cyberattack, and misuse of customer/driver location data: data minimization, tenant isolation, encryption, MFA, least privilege, retention/deletion policy, breach response, DPIA, NDPC compliance, and security testing.
•Routing/provider outages, API price changes, and vendor lock-in: adapter interfaces, cached geocodes where lawful, fallback provider/manual coordinates, quotas, circuit breakers, contract monitoring, and exportable operational data.
•Unit economics and regulatory exposure from operating transport or payments: start as software plus marketplace-neutral integrations, measure contribution margin by zone/segment, avoid owning fleet initially, obtain legal review for courier/transport requirements, and never provide regulated payment/escrow services without licensed partners.
MVP scope
•Multi-tenant merchant and operator console with users, roles, service zones, rate cards, delivery windows, and audit history.
•Canonical order API plus CSV/manual import and signed webhooks; merchant order lifecycle, idempotency, and exception queue.
•Nigeria-specific delivery location capture using phone, landmark, notes, map pin/plus code, confidence, and recipient availability; simple geocoding adapter.
•Dispatch board for manual assignment and basic zone/time-window batching; driver/partner roster and availability.
•Offline-capable Android driver flow for pickup, en route, arrived, failed attempt, delivered, OTP/photo/signature proof, COD amount, and sync retry.
•Customer and merchant notifications through one SMS provider plus a pluggable WhatsApp connector; public tracking link with tokenized access.
•Daily settlement/reporting slice: COD and payment reconciliation import, partner payout export, on-time/first-attempt/failure dashboards, monitoring, backups, and production security baseline.
Expansion path
Start with Lagos same-city B2B and social-commerce deliveries, where density supports route learning and paid pilots. Add Abuja, Ibadan, and Kano through zone templates and partner fleets, then cover intercity line-haul handoffs and warehouse/returns workflows. Next sell the control plane to African 3PLs and multi-location merchants in Ghana, Kenya, South Africa, and francophone West Africa by keeping country adapters separate for tax, messaging, payments, privacy, maps, and courier rules. Use the same core event model and APIs to expand globally into other fragmented-address, cash/transfer, and contractor-fleet markets; partner with local carriers and payment providers rather than owning assets. This sequence is design judgment, not a sourced forecast; AfCFTA/ECOWAS can be considered an expansion enabler, but cross-border customs, FX, licensing, and data-transfer requirements must be validated country by country.
